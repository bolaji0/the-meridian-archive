A map is a kind of promise.

Written in the voice
of Mara Vey

Original concept essay

Every map begins with an omission. Before the coast can become a line, someone must decide which coves are too small to matter. Before a city can become a grid, someone must decide what counts as a street. The finished thing looks factual. The decisions beneath it are anything but.

I used to draw the maps for my stories before I wrote the stories themselves. It felt like a responsible kind of preparation: settle the rivers, establish the mountain range, work out how long a journey would take. Then the characters could move through a world that already knew its own shape.

The useful mistake

But the interesting stories always began where the map failed. A road ended where it ought to continue. Two villages disagreed about the river between them. A woman remembered a square that the city insisted had never existed. The map became useful only when it stopped being reliable.

In The Meridian Engine, Iona makes maps as an act of resistance. She draws what is gone. Her work is not a record of the city as it stands, but an argument with the city as it wishes to be remembered. I wanted the reader to feel that a mark on paper could have weight. That it might cost something to erase it.

A world with consequences

A convincing fictional world does not require exhaustive geography. It requires consequences. If there is a mountain, who cannot cross it? If there is a border, whose family does it divide? If a street vanishes overnight, who has to take the long way home?

These are smaller questions than “How does the world work?” They are also more useful ones. A reader can forgive a blank space on the map. It is harder to forgive a world in which nothing matters to anyone.

I still draw maps. But now I leave room for a place I have not understood yet. An unfinished edge. A road whose destination is not a name but a question. Somewhere for the story to surprise me.
